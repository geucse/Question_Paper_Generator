## Course outcomes

- **CO1** — Understand the concept of abstract machines and their power to recognize languages.
- **CO2** — Formulate DFA, RE and FA with output.
- **CO3** — Design CFG and check the language is not CFL.
- **CO4** — Design PDA and convert n-PDA into D-PDA.
- **CO5** — Design and be familiar with Turing machines and computability.
- **CO6** — Formulate finite machines, push down automata and Turing machines for automated functioning of devices.

### Q1

**Long Answer · 6 marks · medium · Create · CO2 · Unit 1: “Deterministic finite Automata (DFA)”**

Design a DFA over {0, 1} that accepts exactly the strings containing an even number of 0s and ending in 1. Give the transition table and state the purpose of each state.


**Model answer:** Track (parity of 0s, last symbol). States: q0 = even 0s, not ending in 1 (start); q1 = even 0s, ending in 1 (accepting); q2 = odd 0s, ending in 0; q3 = odd 0s, ending in 1. On 0: q0->q2, q1->q2, q2->q0, q3->q0. On 1: q0->q1, q1->q1, q2->q3, q3->q3. Accept q1.

### Q2

**MCQ · 1 mark · easy · Understand · CO1 · Unit 1: “Equivalence of NFA and DFA”**

Using the subset construction, what is the maximum number of states of the DFA equivalent to an NFA with 4 states?

- (A) 4
- (B) 8
- (C) 16
- (D) 24

**Answer:** (C) 16

**Model answer:** Each DFA state is a subset of the NFA states: at most 2^4 = 16.

### Q3

**Short Answer · 5 marks · hard · Apply · CO2 · Unit 1: “Minimization of Finite Automata”**

Minimise the DFA with states A (start), B, C, D, E (E accepting) over {0, 1}: A: 0->B, 1->C; B: 0->B, 1->D; C: 0->B, 1->C; D: 0->B, 1->E; E: 0->B, 1->C. Show the partitions and the number of states in the minimal DFA.


**Model answer:** P0 = {A,B,C,D}, {E}. On 1, D goes to E: P1 = {A,B,C}, {D}, {E}. On 1, B goes to D: P2 = {A,C}, {B}, {D}, {E}. P3 = P2 (stable). A and C merge: the minimal DFA has 4 states.

### Q4

**True/False · 2 marks · medium · Analyze · CO1 · Unit 1: “Myhill-Nerode Theorem”**

True or False: The strings a, aa, aaa, ... are pairwise distinguishable with respect to L = {a^n b^n : n >= 0}, so by the Myhill-Nerode theorem L is not regular.


**Answer:** True

**Model answer:** True. a^i b^i is in L but a^j b^i is not for i != j, giving infinitely many equivalence classes, so L is not regular.

### Q5

**Proof · 6 marks · medium · Analyze · CO1 · Unit 2: “Pumping Lemma for regular Languages”**

Use the pumping lemma to prove that L = {a^n b^n : n >= 0} is not regular.


**Model answer:** Assume L regular with pumping length p. Take w = a^p b^p, w = xyz with |xy| <= p, |y| >= 1, so y = a^k (k >= 1). Then x y^2 z = a^(p+k) b^p is not in L, contradicting the lemma. Hence L is not regular.

### Q6

**Short Answer · 3 marks · easy · Apply · CO2 · Unit 2: “Regular expression to FA”**

Write a regular expression for the strings over {0, 1} whose third symbol from the right end is 1, and state the minimum length of a string it accepts.


**Model answer:** (0+1)* 1 (0+1)(0+1); the shortest accepted strings have length 3 (e.g. 100).

### Q7

**Derivation · 6 marks · hard · Apply · CO2 · Unit 2: “Arden Theorem”**

A DFA has states q1 (start and accepting) and q2 over {a, b}: q1 on a -> q1, on b -> q2; q2 on a -> q1, on b -> q2. Using Arden's theorem, derive a regular expression for its language.


**Model answer:** Equations: q1 = e + q1 a + q2 a; q2 = q1 b + q2 b. Arden on q2: q2 = q1 b b*. Substitute: q1 = e + q1 (a + b b* a). Arden: q1 = (a + b b* a)*. The language is the empty string and all strings ending in a.

### Q8

**MCQ · 2 marks · medium · Understand · CO1 · Unit 2: “Closure properties of Regular Languages”**

Regular languages are NOT closed under which of the following operations?

- (A) Union of two languages
- (B) Complement
- (C) Intersection of two languages
- (D) Infinite union

**Answer:** (D) Infinite union

**Model answer:** Every language is an infinite union of singleton (regular) languages, so closure fails for infinite unions.

### Q9

**Case Study · 10 marks · hard · Create · CO2, CO6 · Unit 2: “Moore and Mealy machine”**

> **Case:** A ticket vending controller accepts Rs 5 and Rs 10 coins one at a time. When the amount inserted reaches Rs 15 it outputs a ticket (and returns Rs 5 change if Rs 20 was inserted); otherwise it outputs nothing and waits for the next coin.

Design a Mealy machine for the vending controller in the case, giving its states, inputs, outputs and transition table, and explain how a Moore machine for the same behaviour would differ.


**Model answer:** States S0, S5, S10 (amount held). Inputs 5, 10. S0: 5/none -> S5, 10/none -> S10. S5: 5/none -> S10, 10/ticket -> S0. S10: 5/ticket -> S0, 10/ticket+change -> S0. A Moore machine attaches outputs to states, so it needs extra states such as T (ticket) and TC (ticket + change), and responds one transition later.

### Q10

**Short Answer · 5 marks · medium · Analyze · CO3 · Unit 3: “Ambiguity in Grammar”**

Show that the grammar E -> E + E | E * E | id is ambiguous using the string id + id * id, and explain why ambiguity matters for a compiler.


**Model answer:** Two leftmost derivations / parse trees: one groups id + (id * id), the other (id + id) * id. They give different evaluation orders, so a compiler could compute different meanings; precedence must be encoded in an unambiguous grammar.

### Q11

**Derivation · 5 marks · medium · Apply · CO3 · Unit 3: “Normal forms for CFGs: CNF and GNF”**

Convert the grammar S -> aSb | ab into Chomsky Normal Form, showing each step.


**Model answer:** Introduce A -> a, B -> b: S -> ASB | AB. Break ASB with X -> SB: S -> AX | AB, X -> SB, A -> a, B -> b. All rules are now A -> BC or A -> a.

### Q12

**True/False · 1 mark · easy · Remember · CO3 · Unit 3: “Pumping lemma for CFLs”**

True or False: The language {a^n b^n c^n : n >= 1} is context free.


**Answer:** False

**Model answer:** False. The pumping lemma for CFLs shows it is not context free.

### Q13

**Short Answer · 4 marks · medium · Create · CO3 · Unit 3: “Context free grammar (CFG)”**

Design a context-free grammar for all palindromes over {a, b} and draw the derivation tree for abba.


**Model answer:** S -> aSa | bSb | a | b | e. abba: S => aSa => abSba => abba (S -> e); the tree has root S with children a, S, a; the inner S has children b, S, b; the innermost S derives e.

### Q14

**Long Answer · 8 marks · hard · Create · CO4 · Unit 4: “Acceptance by empty stack”**

Design a PDA that accepts {a^n b^n : n >= 1} by empty stack. Give the transition function and the sequence of instantaneous descriptions for the input aabb.


**Model answer:** States q0, q1; bottom marker Z. d(q0, a, Z) = (q0, AZ); d(q0, a, A) = (q0, AA); d(q0, b, A) = (q1, e); d(q1, b, A) = (q1, e); d(q1, e, Z) = (q1, e). IDs: (q0, aabb, Z) |- (q0, abb, AZ) |- (q0, bb, AAZ) |- (q1, b, AZ) |- (q1, e, Z) |- (q1, e, e): accepted.

### Q15

**MCQ · 2 marks · medium · Analyze · CO4 · Unit 4: “Deterministic PDA”**

Which of these languages cannot be accepted by any deterministic PDA?

- (A) {a^n b^n : n >= 0}
- (B) {w c w^R : w in {a,b}*}
- (C) {w w^R : w in {a,b}*}
- (D) {a^n b^2n : n >= 0}

**Answer:** (C) {w w^R : w in {a,b}*}

**Model answer:** For w w^R the machine cannot know where the middle is without a marker; it needs nondeterminism. w c w^R has the marker c.

### Q16

**Short Answer · 4 marks · medium · Understand · CO4 · Unit 4: “Equivalence of PDA and CFG”**

Explain how a PDA accepting by empty stack is constructed from a CFG, and why it simulates leftmost derivations.


**Model answer:** One state; start with S on the stack. For each variable A and rule A -> x: an e-move replacing A by x on the stack. For each terminal a: read a and pop a. The stack holds the unread part of a sentential form, expanding the leftmost variable each time, so it follows a leftmost derivation.

### Q17

**Assertion-Reason · 2 marks · medium · Understand · CO4, CO5 · Unit 4: “Two stack PDA”**

Read the assertion and reason about two stack PDA and choose the correct option.

- **Assertion (A):** A PDA with two stacks is as powerful as a Turing machine.
- **Reason (R):** The two stacks can hold the tape contents to the left and to the right of the head.
- (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
- (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
- (C) A is true, but R is false
- (D) A is false, but R is true

**Answer:** (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A

**Model answer:** Both true and R explains A: moving the head is popping one stack and pushing the other.

### Q18

**Proof · 8 marks · hard · Evaluate · CO5 · Unit 5: “Halting problem”**

Prove that the halting problem for Turing machines is undecidable.


**Model answer:** Suppose H(M, w) decides whether M halts on w. Build D(M): run H(M, M); if it says 'halts', loop forever, else halt. D(D) halts iff H(D, D) says it does not halt: contradiction. So no such H exists.

### Q19

**Short Answer · 3 marks · easy · Understand · CO5 · Unit 5: “Universal TM”**

What is a universal Turing machine, and why is it considered a model of the stored-program computer?


**Model answer:** A TM U that takes an encoding <M, w> and simulates M on w. Like a stored-program computer, the program (M) is data on the tape, so one machine runs any program.

### Q20

**Short Answer · 4 marks · medium · Apply · CO5 · Unit 5: “Post correspondence problem (PCP)”**

For the PCP instance A = (1, 10111, 10) and B = (111, 10, 0), find a solution sequence of indices and show that both concatenations are equal.


**Model answer:** Indices 2, 1, 1, 3: A gives 10111 1 1 10 = 101111110; B gives 10 111 111 0 = 101111110.
